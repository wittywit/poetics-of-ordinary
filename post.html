On the texture of a doorknob after a thousand touches

Pranshu Chaudhary · March 2026 · ~5 min read · Doorknobs · Touch · Habit

We grip it without thinking. The hand knows the weight before the mind does — a smooth brass globe at the end of a lever, unchanged in thirty years, worn at the equator where a thousand palms have rested. When was the last time you actually felt it?

The Method

For seven days I tried to touch the front door handle of my apartment as if for the first time. This is harder than it sounds. The brain does not cooperate. Familiarity is not merely a feeling — it is an active suppression. The nervous system, trained on efficiency, refuses the signal. The hand reaches, turns, the door opens, and you are already somewhere else in your thoughts before the metal has left your palm.

The practice required what I can only call deliberate friction — a manufactured pause, inserted before every contact, during which I would consciously acknowledge what was about to happen. Not anticipate. Acknowledge. The distinction matters.

The familiar is not a quality of the object. It is a quality of attention — or rather, of its absence.

Over the first three days, the exercise felt theatrical. I stood in my own doorway, hand extended, trying to feel something that was already in my hand. The brass was neither warm nor cold; it was whatever temperature the room had made it. It fit my grip exactly, because my grip had formed around it over years of daily contact. The object had been shaped by my use of it. And yet I could not feel it at all.

Field Notes

Session 1 — Day 1, 6:47am

Temperature noticeably cooler than expected — the metal has not yet taken the morning's warmth. The resistance before the latch releases is not uniform; there is a micro-pause, a catch, before the mechanism fully gives. I had never noticed the pause. It has presumably been there every single time.

Session 3 — Day 3, 11:30pm

After three days of deliberate attention, the handle has begun to feel strange again. This is the target condition — what the Russian Formalists called ostranenie, defamiliarisation. The goal was not to make the doorknob interesting. The goal was to restore its actual thingness, which had been overwritten by habit.

Session 7 — Day 7, 3:12pm

The worn equator is visible if you look: a band of slightly lighter brass where the lacquer has been completely removed by palms. The top and bottom of the knob retain a faint finish. Only the contact zone has been taken back to bare metal. This is a record of touching. A geological layer made of use.

By the end of the week I had begun to notice other doorknobs — in shops, in the offices of friends, on public buildings. The variation in weight alone was startling. A knob in a government building so heavy it required the wrist, not just the fingers. A café door so light it offered no resistance at all, no sense of a mechanism behind it. A knob in a friend's old flat that turned but required a second, firmer push — a small negotiation the body had presumably learned and performed automatically each time.

What Remains Unknown

The most interesting question this enquiry raised is one I cannot yet answer: at what point does familiarity become loss? There is something necessary about the brain's capacity to automate. If we felt the full weight of every surface we touched, every sound we heard, every visual field we moved through, the experience of daily life would be unnavigable. Defamiliarisation is not a return to a better state — it is a temporary intervention, a diagnostic procedure. You open the instrument briefly to see what is inside, then close it again.

What remains unknown is whether repeated defamiliarisation changes the baseline — whether, after enough deliberate attention, the hand retains something of what it has noticed, even in ordinary use. I suspect it does. I suspect that seven days of deliberate touching has permanently altered, in some small way, how I reach for that door. But this is not something that can be measured. It can only be attended to.

Note A

The Russian Formalist concept of ostranenie — "making strange" — was introduced by Viktor Shklovsky in 1917. Art exists, he argued, to restore the sensation of life; to make the stone feel stony.

Note B

The worn equator of a brass doorknob is a form of indexical sign — it points to the action that produced it. Like a footpath worn across grass: proof of the desire line.

Note C

Maurice Merleau-Ponty: "The body is our general medium for having a world." The enquiry into objects is always, simultaneously, an enquiry into the body that uses them.

On Method

Sessions were conducted at varying times of day to account for thermal variation in the object and in the hands of the observer. Notes taken immediately after contact, not during.
